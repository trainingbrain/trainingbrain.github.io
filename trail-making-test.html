The Science of the Trail Making Test (TMT Part A & Part B)

Originally introduced in 1944 as part of the Army Individual Test Battery and later incorporated into the prestigious Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery by Ralph Reitan in 1958, the Trail Making Test (TMT) is one of the most widely administered clinical assessments in neurology, geriatric medicine, and neuropsychology.

Part A vs. Part B: What Do They Test?

  • TMT Part A (Numbers 1 to 15): Measures visual search speed, sustained attention, and motor-visual coordination. Participants connect numbered circles in sequential ascending order as fast as possible.
  • TMT Part B (Alternating 1-A-2-B-3-C...): Requires simultaneous maintenance of two independent mental sequences (numerical and alphabetical). It tests executive cognitive shifting, divided attention, and cognitive flexibility.

Clinical Significance of the B/A Ratio

In clinical neurology, the ratio of time taken to complete Part B relative to Part A ($B / A$) isolates pure executive task-switching cost from baseline motor or visual slowing:

  • Ratio < 2.5: Efficient cognitive flexibility and task shifting.
  • Ratio > 3.0: Suggests disproportionate executive shifting difficulty or frontal lobe fatigue, independent of visual processing speed.
